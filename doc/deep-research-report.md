# Passenger Noise Data Inside London Underground Trains

## What is actually available

The short answer is that **useful data does exist**, but it is fragmented, inconsistently published, and rarely released in machine-readable form. The strongest public evidence comes from three families of material: **TfL technical reports released through FOI**, which often contain inter-station A-weighted levels and sometimes section-by-section appendices; **academic studies**, which add passenger-centred measurements and, in one recent case, correlations with track geometry; and **citizen-science maps and spreadsheets**, which broaden coverage but have weaker metadata and licensing. TfL also makes clear that it measures noise and duration across Underground lines and continues to use grinding, lubrication, alternative fastenings, speed variation trials, and tuned mass dampers to manage the problem, but those network-wide measurements are not published as an open dataset. citeturn21view0turn24view3turn33view2turn33view0

For an open-source **Tube interior noise map**, the most valuable currently-public sources are the **Central line passenger-car surveys and test-train report**, the **Bakerloo passenger-car survey**, the **Northern line passenger and driver surveys**, and the **Stratford-Leyton grinding/noise history disclosed in a 2025 FOI response**. These give genuine route-section acoustics rather than strategic environmental contour mapping, which you specifically excluded. They are sufficient to bootstrap a **partial, evidence-backed section map** for parts of the Central, Bakerloo and Northern lines, but they are **not enough to cover the whole network** without new measurements. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn31view0turn29view1turn25view0

A crucial practical point is licensing. **TfL FOI attachments are public to read, but they are not released through a clear open-data licence on the release page itself**; TfL repeatedly points requesters to separate copyright and re-use guidance. In practice, that makes them highly valuable for research, benchmarking and manual extraction, but awkward for clean open-data redistribution unless TfL explicitly grants reuse. By contrast, **TfL station-location open data is under the Open Government Licence on the London Datastore**, **OpenStreetMap/OpenRailwayMap data is under ODbL**, and the **2025 MDPI paper is CC BY**. citeturn31view0turn25view0turn38search6turn40search3turn40search2turn43view0

### What these TfL releases show overall

Taken together, the FOI releases show that **TfL possesses much richer internal acoustics data than it routinely publishes**. The 2023-2025 releases demonstrate measurements at **passenger position**, **cab position**, **inter-station section**, **whole-route**, and **track-location-code** resolution. They also show that TfL tracks not just sound level but, in some cases, **speed**, **one-third-octave spectra**, and **pre/post-maintenance changes**. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn21view2turn7view1turn25view0

The strongest open mapping opportunity today is therefore a **patchwork map built from released section-level surveys**, rather than waiting for a single official network dataset. The biggest current limitation is coverage: released passenger-car data is excellent on some lines and missing on others, while Victoria and some Northern/Central releases are **cab-focused** rather than passenger-saloon focused. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn31view0turn45view0

### What these non-TfL sources add

The academic material matters because it captures the **passenger experience directly**, rather than occupational cab exposure only. The 2015 and 2020 studies are especially useful as **historical anchors**, and the 2025 Northern line paper is important because it shows that **consumer devices plus careful signal processing can recover meaningful hotspot patterns and link them to track geometry**. citeturn44view0turn19search14turn43view0

The citizen-science material matters for a different reason: it shows there is already public appetite to measure this problem, and it provides rough templates for **route-level map presentation**, **device placement choices**, and **community collection campaigns**. The weakness is that neither the EAVE campaign nor the Tech Forum spreadsheet is packaged like a durable public dataset with clear metadata, calibration lineage or open licensing. citeturn35view0turn35view1turn37view0

## Proxy datasets and historical engineering signals

### The most useful proxy layers

When direct section-level passenger measurements are absent, the next best predictors are the ones most clearly tied to wheel-rail noise generation: **corrugation**, **curvature**, **cant / versine**, **speed profile**, **lubrication state**, **fastening or pad type**, and **maintenance history**. TfL’s own public material is unusually helpful here. Its track standards explicitly define **corrugation limits** and inspection actions, including thresholds that trigger examination for grinding or replacement, and its maintenance standard states that failure to maintain rail lubricators can lead to wheel screech and a potential noise hazard for customers. citeturn12view3turn12view4

The strongest public engineering proxy is the 2021 paper by Andy Vickerstaff on **automatic train operation and track infrastructure**. It includes charts of **rail roughness growth**, **speed distributions**, discussion of how repeated traction/braking and curving drive corrugation growth, and explicit evidence that **grinding interventions reduced rail roughness** at the studied sites. It also explains that low-resilience deep-tube track forms and specific sleeper/baseplate arrangements can worsen airborne in-tunnel noise even while trying to reduce ground-borne vibration to neighbours. citeturn14view1turn14view2turn14view3

TfL’s current public noise page and corporate panel papers add the operational layer. TfL says it spends about **£150 million a year on track improvements**, including a **£1 million annual budget for rail-noise technology**, and uses **rail grinding, worn-rail replacement, rail-joint fixes, track lubrication, resilient fixings, speed variations, tuned mass dampers, and replacement of fastenings linked to increased in-carriage noise**. In 2024 TfL said a **hybrid acoustic grinding train** would be trialled on the Northern and Victoria lines in early 2025, and that **Delkor fastenings are now fitted as standard in ongoing Deep Tube track renewal** after trial success. citeturn33view2turn33view0turn23view0

### Historic reports worth mining

Historic TfL and GLA material is not as immediately map-ready, but it is still useful. TfL’s **2003 environmental report** said a **predictive monitoring tool** was being developed to forecast rail deterioration so maintenance could be planned before noise worsened, and the **2004 environmental report** described rail grinding, lubrication and track monitoring as ongoing controls for wheel-rail noise. A 2004 TfL agenda paper also referred to a **limited noise-monitoring programme** and to known wheel-squeal locations being lubricated and provided with continuously welded track. citeturn42search13turn42search6turn42search11

The **Kennington Loop** material is also worth attention. A 2016 FOI attachment says planned noise-reduction work on the loop included **re-railing, replacing resilient pads and hand-grinding**, and later Northern line occupational reports include an explicit **Kennington Loop** journey with inter-station values. A 2017 London Assembly answer additionally records TfL’s plan to install **shock-absorbent fixings** and take **follow-up measurements** at affected properties. That combination makes Kennington a promising historical case study for linking track interventions to measured outcomes. citeturn28search1turn29view1turn31view1

Finally, the London Transport Museum archive guides are worth keeping in scope. The 2024 **Health and Safety Records** guide points to files on **asbestos noise-reduction equipment in tube tunnels**, defective tracks and rail joints, accident/incident reports, and wider operational records. Those are more archive leads than datasets, but they could uncover historic section-level or technology-specific noise evidence otherwise absent from the web. citeturn41view0

## The most practical open-source architecture

### What can be bootstrapped now

A realistic first release should be a **segmented route map**, not a falsely precise continuous heatmap. The base network can be built from **official station points and stop IDs** from TfL, **weekly timetable feeds** for scheduled inter-station timings, and **OpenStreetMap/OpenRailwayMap geometry** for the railway linework. That combination is practical because TfL’s current public feeds are good for stations and timetables, while recent Tech Forum guidance says precise Underground geoline coordinates are not available from the Unified API for Tube routes. citeturn38search1turn38search6turn39search0turn38search5turn40search2turn40search3

On top of that network, the first data layer should ingest all released **direct-measurement sections** as canonical observations. In practice that means: Central line 92TS passenger-car sections; Bakerloo 72TS sections; the Northern line passenger corridor around Euston-Camden-Tufnell Park; the Northern line cab/inter-station reports including Kennington Loop; the Stratford-Leyton grind-history time series; and the 2025 Central line test-vehicle report with LCS track references. Each observation should carry metadata for **measurement position** (seated / standing / cab), **vehicle stock**, **date**, **direction**, **duration**, **device class**, and **source confidence**. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn31view0turn29view1turn25view0turn7view1

That alone would already support an open map with three strong modes: **measured**, **measured but occupational proxy**, and **estimated**. That distinction is important because the public evidence is not uniformly passenger-saloon data. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn21view2turn45view0

### What needs new measurement

The current public record is still too sparse for a trustworthy network-wide passenger map. Missing areas include most of the **Victoria, Jubilee, Piccadilly, Waterloo & City, Metropolitan, Circle, District and Hammersmith & City** lines at passenger position. Even where TfL clearly holds internal evidence, FOI responses show that disclosure is selective rather than systematic. citeturn21view0turn45view0turn24view3

So the second layer should be a **modelled estimator**, trained on measured sections and fed by proxy variables that the public record strongly ties to noise generation: **curve radius**, **cant / versine**, **scheduled run time**, **speed-management regime**, **known grinding recency**, **trackform / fastening family**, and **line type** such as deep tube versus subsurface. This is not guesswork masquerading as truth: the 2025 Northern line study found clear relationships between tighter curves / larger cant and higher noise, and the ATO/corrugation work plus TfL standards show why corrugation, speed distribution and fastening type matter. citeturn43view0turn14view2turn14view3turn12view3turn33view0

### How crowdsourced smartphones should fill the gap

Crowdsourcing is the only plausible way to fill London-wide passenger coverage without waiting for TfL to publish a complete internal dataset. The right design is **not** “everyone uploads any dB reading”. It should be a constrained protocol:

Use an open or auditable app stack such as **NoiseCapture**, **OpeNoise**, or a project-specific wrapper, and borrow the discipline seen in the 2025 Northern line study: fixed device placement, repeated runs, direction labels, stock identification where possible, and temporal segmentation rather than a single journey average. NoiseCapture and OpeNoise already provide open smartphone measurement tooling, while the Northern line paper shows smartphone collection can be scientifically useful when its protocol is tight. citeturn15search4turn15search8turn43view0

The project should ask contributors to capture only a few structured fields beyond the dB trace: **line, direction, start and end stations, carriage position, approximate crowding, phone model, and whether the phone was handheld, pocketed or resting on the carriage**. Those metadata are simple enough for volunteers and crucial for later normalisation. The output geometry should then snap to the canonical inter-station graph rather than trusting raw GPS in deep tunnels. That inference is supported by the fact that official station locations and route sequences / timetables already exist, while exact Underground geoline coordinates in official feeds are limited. citeturn38search6turn39search0turn38search5

A practical publication model would therefore have three confidence tiers. **Tier A** would be directly measured official or academic sections. **Tier B** would be repeated crowdsourced sections with strong agreement across runs. **Tier C** would be model estimates from the proxy engine. That architecture is both scientifically honest and achievable with today’s public materials. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn43view0turn25view0

### Recommended implementation stack

The most practical stack is a **PostGIS-backed segment database** keyed by canonical inter-station and sub-section IDs. Use **TfL station IDs and timetable feeds** for topology and time, **OSM/OpenRailwayMap** for geometry, store direct observations from FOI and academic sources as immutable measurement records, and generate a derived **section exposure index** that can output in both **dBA** and **time-to-threshold style metrics** for public communication. The front end should default to section colours with metadata pop-ups showing whether a value is measured or inferred, when it was last measured, and what intervention history is known for that section. citeturn38search6turn39search0turn40search2turn25view0turn44view0

If I were prioritising effort, I would do it in this order: first, digitise and normalise the released **Central, Bakerloo and Northern** measurements; second, attach **maintenance and proxy layers** such as grinding recency, corrugation thresholds and geometry; third, launch a **structured crowdsourcing campaign** on the missing lines; and fourth, use new observations to retrain the estimator and steadily replace inferred values with measured ones. That path is the fastest route to an open-source Tube interior noise map that is useful, transparent and defensible. citeturn9view2turn9view3turn29view1turn25view0turn12view3turn43view0
